\begin{abstract}
Under the FIDE Laws of Chess, which end a game automatically on a fivefold
repetition or when 75 moves by each player pass without a pawn move or a
capture, the length of a chess game is bounded. Explicit games of
$17{,}697$ plies ($8{,}848\tfrac12$ moves) had been published, and a
construction-based count allowed $17{,}699$ plies; to our knowledge, no
published argument established that ceiling over all games or excluded
the lengths $17{,}698$ and $17{,}699$. We prove that
the maximum length of a chess game is exactly $17{,}697$ plies. The upper
bound is elementary: a pawn move or a capture resets the 75-move counter,
these moves cut a game into at most $118$ segments, a segment holds at
most $150$ plies, and one further ply is lost each time the colour making
the segment-ending moves changes. The heart of the proof shows that a
game attaining all $118$ segments must change that colour at least three
times, so that no game exceeds $150 \cdot 118 - 3 = 17{,}697$ plies; the
equality analysis further forces every maximum-length game to promote all
sixteen pawns, fill every segment, and change colour exactly three times.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and prior work}\label{sec:intro}

How long can a game of chess be? Under earlier versions of the Laws the
question had no finite answer: the draw by threefold repetition and the
50-move rule must be \emph{claimed} by a player, so two cooperating players
could continue indefinitely. The unboundedness is classical: Euwe
\cite{euwe1929} exhibited endless play under the repetition rules of his
day by rediscovering the cube-free Thue--Morse sequence, an analysis
taken up in symbolic dynamics by Morse and Hedlund
\cite{morsehedlund1944}. This changed on 1 July 2014, when FIDE
introduced two automatic termination rules \cite{fide2014laws}, retained in
the current Laws \cite{fide2023laws}: the game is drawn, without any claim, when the same
position occurs five times (Article~9.6.1), or when $75$ consecutive moves
by each player have been made without the movement of any pawn and without
any capture (Article~9.6.2)\footnote{The bound of $75$ has a history of
its own: during the twentieth century the \emph{claimable} 50-move limit
was repeatedly extended for particular endings --- at one stage to
exactly $75$ moves for six listed endgames --- before all exceptions
were abolished; see the entry ``fifty-move law'' of \cite{hooperwhyld}.
The automatic rule of 2014 is a separate, claim-independent mechanism.}
--- with the proviso that a checkmate delivered
by the final move takes precedence. The clock rules have teeth: the
seven-piece endgame tablebases \cite{lomonosov2019} contain forced
checkmates more than five hundred moves long \cite{top8dtm}, wins that
Article~9.6.2 erases. Under these Laws the supremum of game
lengths is finite, and computing it exactly becomes a well-posed
combinatorial problem --- an extremal companion to the older
quantitative questions about chess: Shannon's estimates of the number of
games \cite{shannon1950}, the counting and bounding of positions
\cite{steinerberger2015,gourion2022,tromppositions}, the complexity of
the generalised game \cite{fraenkel1981}.

The question is older than the automatic rules. For the claim-based
50-move setting --- under the assumption that the claimable draws are in
fact invoked --- the answer was worked out by chess problemists over half
a century: Dawson published the limit of $5{,}900$ moves in 1911
\cite{dawson1911}, maximum-length problems by Sunyer and H{\o}eg brought
the attainable value down to $5{,}898$, and Fabel's finer analysis of
1947 settled the claim-based maximum at $5{,}898\tfrac12$ moves
\cite{fabel1947}; Britten and later Roycroft treated the variant in
which the two bare kings play on, as reported in \cite{bonsdorff1974}.
Heimo's bilingual account \cite{heimo2004} re-examined the claim-based
maximum under the Laws of 2004 --- finding $5{,}898$ whole moves, an
exchange over the dead-position article adjusting his final schedule ---
and closed by observing that with an odd bound ``such as 75'' in place
of $50$ the answer would become White's $8{,}849$th move: $17{,}697$
plies, ten years before the 75-move rule existed. The note sketches a
corresponding construction, but for a hypothetical claim-based rule; we
have not found in it an argument that every legal game must lose at
least three plies, and the automatic termination rules under which the
question becomes unconditional did not yet exist.
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} supplies that missing universal upper bound. We
follow the
account in the mathematical-chess collection of Bonsdorff, Fabel and
Riihimaa \cite{bonsdorff1974}, where the original sources are cited and
the count of $118$ draw-postponing opportunities --- $96$ pawn moves plus
$22$ captures not already among them --- is displayed explicitly. That
count is the one every later analysis, this paper's included, rests on;
what the automatic rules changed is the arithmetic built on it.

For the modern automatic rules the value itself is also not new, and its
ingredients were assembled in public. The count of $118$ reappeared on the
Chess.com forums in 2011, together with the parity correction for
switching colour --- then taken to cost two full moves, via four
switches \cite{forum2011}; Falkenbach found a three-switch plan for the
50-move setting in 2012 \cite{forum2011}; and within a year of the
75-move rule taking effect, Tatzelwurm and Deedlit redid the arithmetic
under it, Deedlit exhibiting a three-switch schedule and putting its
length at $17{,}697$ plies \cite{forum2015}. Labelle
\cite{labelle2015longest} collected and sharpened this in 2015: counting
the $118$ \emph{delay plies}, crediting the schedules, generating a
complete $17{,}697$-ply game, and stating, as a belief supported by the
constructions, that the maximum is
$118 \times 75.0 - 3 \times 0.5 = 8{,}848\tfrac12$ moves; his page also
records the claim-based value $5{,}898\tfrac12$, agreeing with Fabel.
Murphy \cite{murphy2020longest} later published a separate, hand-designed
complete game of the same length --- the \emph{reference game} verified
in Appendix~\ref{app:witness} --- together with a construction-based
count allowing $17{,}699$ plies, but not a proof that this ceiling
holds for arbitrary games, and posed the exact
maximum as an open question: ``We
clearly need at least one switch \dots\ but is it possible to do it with
only two? If not, can this lower bound be proved?'' Tromp's reference
note \cite{tromp-longest} records the value, and Bolton's talk
\cite{bolton2021} presents the construction. The explicit games of
\cite{labelle2015longest} and \cite{murphy2020longest} establish the
lower bound $17{,}697$; no published argument excludes $17{,}698$ or
$17{,}699$, and to our knowledge no proof of optimality has appeared.
This paper supplies one:

\begin{mainresult}
Under the usual rules of chess --- checkmate, stalemate, dead positions
--- together with the automatic fivefold-repetition and 75-move draws in
force since 2014, no legal game lasts more than $17{,}697$ plies
(Theorem~\ref{thm:main}); the bound is attained by the published games,
so the maximum length of a chess game is exactly $17{,}697$ plies
(Corollary~\ref{cor:exact}).
\end{mainresult}

In the notation of the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences, the
result says precisely that the game-counting sequence A048987
\cite{oeisA048987} has finite support, with last nonzero term
$a(17697)$.

Relative to the prior analyses the new content lies in the upper bound,
and it is twofold. First, the count of $118$ must be turned into a bound
over \emph{all} games: a game need not promote every pawn, so the
deduction of eight overlapping moves cannot be read off the intended
construction and must be derived --- Proposition~\ref{prop:pminuso}
couples the pawn-move and overlap counts through the number of resolved
files, giving $P - O \le 88$ for every game --- and the thirtieth
capture interacts with the end of the game (Lemma~\ref{lem:ct30}).
Second, and this is the bulk of the paper, the three half-move losses
are proved unavoidable: no ordering of the critical moves of any
$118$-segment game achieves fewer than three colour changes
(Theorem~\ref{thm:s3}). The equality analysis comes free and is recorded
as Corollary~\ref{cor:structure}: a maximum-length game promotes all
sixteen
pawns, fills every segment to its target length, and changes colour
exactly three times --- always in the same order, its critical-move and
endpoint patterns both being $\mathsf{BWBW}$.

\paragraph{The shape of the proof.}
A \emph{critical move} --- a pawn move or a capture --- resets the 75-move
counter and is irreversible. Critical moves cut a game into \emph{segments},
each holding at most $149$ reversible plies plus the critical move that ends
it, so a game with $K$ segments has at most $150K$ plies; a parity argument
lowers this bound by one further ply each time the \emph{colour} making the
segment-ending moves changes, giving $L \le 150K - S$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:decomposition}). Counting pawn moves and captures yields
$K \le 118$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:k118}), and $150 \cdot 118 - 1 = 17{,}699$ is
the ceiling of \cite{murphy2020longest}. The remaining two plies are
removed in Section~\ref{sec:switches}: a game attaining $K = 118$ is so
constrained --- each of the sixteen pawns must make six pawn moves,
every one advancing a single rank, and promote, and at least $29$
captures must occur --- that its critical
moves cannot be scheduled with fewer than three colour changes
(Theorem~\ref{thm:s3}). Four of the six candidate patterns are
eliminated by counting alone; the two three-block patterns are
eliminated by a lemma about
pawns: \emph{while the enemy's pawns all stand on their home rank, an
advancing pawn stops four moves short of promotion}
(Lemma~\ref{lem:homerank}). Since $150 \cdot 117 = 17{,}550 < 17{,}697$,
games with $K \le 117$ are shorter still, and the theorem follows.

Nothing in the bound depends on how the game ends, which is why one argument
covers checkmate, the 75-move rule, fivefold repetition, stalemate and
\emph{dead positions} alike --- the last being the Laws' own term for a
position from which no sequence of legal moves can checkmate either side;
the game is drawn the moment one arises (Article~5.2.2
\cite{fide2023laws}). That article has a literature of its own: it
created the \emph{dead reckoning} genre of retrograde-analysis problems
\cite{turnbull2001,buchanan2001}, has its own article in the codex of
chess composition \cite{wfcccodex}, and deciding it algorithmically is a
published problem in its own right \cite{ambrona2022}. The proof below
uses it exactly once, for king against king (Lemma~\ref{lem:ct30}).

\paragraph{Computer verification.}
The proof is a hand argument and is meant to be checked by hand;
independently of it, every finite case analysis in the text was
exhausted by computer, the reference game was verified ply by ply
(Appendix~\ref{app:witness}), and the central case analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:switches} was cross-checked by two additional
implementations (Appendix~\ref{app:crosschecks}). The companion
repository \cite{repo} packages these checks, with a claims-to-code map
(\texttt{CLAIMS.md}) and a byte-for-byte--reproducible certificate.
Computation verifies the known construction and cross-checks the proof;
it does not replace it.

\section{Setting, critical moves, and the segment decomposition}
\label{sec:setting}

\subsection{The rules assumed}\label{sec:rules}

A \emph{game} is a sequence of legal moves from the standard initial
position, played until a rule of the FIDE Laws \cite{fide2023laws} ends it.
We write $L$ for its length in \emph{plies} --- half-moves, single moves
by one player --- and number plies from $1$; White plays the odd plies.
The game-ending rules relevant here are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Checkmate} (Art.~5.1.1) and \emph{stalemate} (Art.~5.2.1) end
    the game on the move that produces them.
  \item \emph{Dead position} (Art.~5.2.2): the game is drawn the moment a
    position arises from which no sequence of legal moves can lead to a
    checkmate by either side.
  \item \emph{Fivefold repetition} (Art.~9.6.1): the game is drawn, with no
    claim required, when the same position (same pieces on the same squares,
    same side to move, same castling and en passant possibilities, as
    specified in Art.~9.2) has occurred five times.\footnote{The 2014 formulation
    required the five occurrences to be consecutive; the requirement was
    removed in the 2017 revision \cite{fide2017laws}. The difference is
    immaterial here: the upper bound follows from the automatic
    75-move rule alone, and the verified reference game never lets any
    position occur even three times (Appendix~\ref{app:witness}).}
  \item \emph{Seventy-five--move rule} (Art.~9.6.2): the game is drawn, with
    no claim required, when at least $75$ consecutive moves by each player
    --- that is, $150$ consecutive plies --- have been played without any
    pawn move and without any capture. If the ply completing the $150$
    delivers checkmate, the checkmate stands.
\end{enumerate}
Draws by agreement, resignations, and the \emph{claimable} draws (threefold
repetition, Art.~9.2; the 50-move rule, Art.~9.3) are voluntary: players
maximising the length of a game simply never invoke them, so they play no
role in the maximisation. This is the setting of \cite{murphy2020longest}.

It is convenient to track the \emph{halfmove clock} $\clock(t)$: the number
of plies played since the most recent pawn move or capture (or since the
start of the game, if there has been none). Article~9.6.2 says precisely
that the game ends when $\clock$ reaches $150$, the ply that brings it there
being the last of the game, and being scored as mate if it mates.

\subsection{Critical moves and segments}

\begin{definition}[Critical move]\label{def:critical}
A \emph{critical move} is a pawn move or a capture. (En passant is both; a
promotion is a pawn move; castling is neither.) A \emph{quiet} move is a
move that is not critical.
\end{definition}

Critical moves are exactly the moves that reset the halfmove clock, and they
are irreversible: a pawn cannot retreat, and a captured piece never
returns. We keep the term of \cite{murphy2020longest}, where the problem
was posed: ``Let's call an irreversible move that resets the 75-move
counter a \emph{critical move}.'' Labelle \cite{labelle2015longest}
calls the same plies \emph{delay plies}; the usage is unrelated to the
colloquial sense of a tactically critical move.

\begin{definition}[Segments, endpoints, $K$, $T$]\label{def:segments}
Let $t_1 < t_2 < \dots < t_m$ be the plies of a game's critical moves. Set
$T = 1$ if quiet plies follow the last critical move (or if the game has
moves but no critical move at all), and $T = 0$ otherwise. The game's
\emph{endpoints} are
\[
  e_i = t_i \ (1 \le i \le m), \qquad e_{m+1} = L \text{ if } T = 1,
\]
together with the virtual endpoint $e_0 = 0$. The number of \emph{segments}
is $K = m + T$; segment $i$ consists of plies $e_{i-1}+1, \dots, e_i$.
Every ply lies in exactly one segment, and $e_K = L$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Switches $S$]\label{def:switches}
The \emph{colour} of an endpoint $e_i$ ($i \ge 1$) is the colour of the
player who played ply $e_i$ --- White if $e_i$ is odd, Black if even. By
convention the virtual endpoint $e_0 = 0$ is \emph{Black} (it has the parity
of a Black ply, and the game opens as if Black had just made a critical
move). The switch count is
\[
  S \;=\; \#\{\, i \in \{1,\dots,K\} : \text{colour}(e_i) \ne
  \text{colour}(e_{i-1}) \,\}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Segment decomposition]\label{lem:decomposition}
Every game satisfies
\[
  L \;=\; 150K - S - \sum_{i=1}^{K}\delta_i
  \qquad\text{with every } \delta_i \ge 0,
\]
where $\delta_i$ is the shortfall of segment $i$ from its target length
($150$ if endpoints $e_{i-1},e_i$ have the same colour, $149$ otherwise).
In particular $L \le 150K - S$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix $i$ and consider the segment plies $e_{i-1}+1, \dots, e_i$. All of them
except possibly the last are quiet ($t_{i}$ is the next critical ply after
$t_{i-1}$; the terminal segment is entirely quiet). Suppose
$e_i - e_{i-1} \ge 151$. Then the $150$ plies
$e_{i-1}+1, \dots, e_{i-1}+150$ are all quiet, so after ply $e_{i-1}+150$
the halfmove clock reads $150$ and the game has ended there by
Article~9.6.2 (as a draw, or as the mate that takes precedence --- either
way it has ended), contradicting the existence of ply
$e_{i-1}+151 \le e_i \le L$. Hence $e_i - e_{i-1}\le 150$.

Plies alternate colours, so $e_i - e_{i-1}$ is even exactly when $e_i$ and
$e_{i-1}$ have the same colour; this is where the convention
$\text{colour}(e_0) = $ Black is used, $0$ having the parity of a Black
ply. A segment whose endpoints differ in colour therefore has odd length,
and being $\le 150$, has length $\le 149$. Summing the segment lengths
telescopes to $e_K - e_0 = L$ and yields the identity.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The terminal quiet segment genuinely can reach $150$ plies: if the game
ends by the 75-move rule, the drawing ply is the $150$th quiet ply after
the last critical move and it \emph{is played}. Its parity then forces the
terminal endpoint to have the same colour as the previous one, consistent
with the accounting above. A terminal segment ending earlier --- by a quiet
checkmate, a stalemate, a fivefold repetition or a dead position --- is
only shorter.
\end{remark}

Counting critical moves by inclusion--exclusion, with $P$ the number of pawn
moves, $C$ the number of captures and $O$ the number of \emph{overlaps}
(moves that are both, i.e.\ diagonal pawn captures, en passant included),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:K}
  K \;=\; (P - O) + (C + T).
\end{equation}
The next two sections bound the two halves of \eqref{eq:K} separately.
Neither half refers to how the game ends.

\section{At most 118 critical segments}\label{sec:k118}

Throughout, we count over the $32$ \emph{pieces} that begin the game ---
each one followed through the whole game, keeping its identity under
promotion --- rather than over piece types; ``piece'' is used inclusively
here, so pawns are pieces too. A piece that begins as a pawn is still that
same piece after promoting.

\begin{lemma}[Pawn basics]\label{lem:pawnbasics}
Each pawn makes at most $6$ pawn moves, so $P \le 96$. A pawn that makes
exactly $6$ pawn moves advances exactly one rank with each of them and
promotes.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Every pawn move advances the pawn at least one rank toward promotion, and
there are six ranks between its home rank and the promotion rank. A pawn
making six moves therefore advances exactly one rank per move and finishes
on the promotion rank, where promotion is mandatory.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Origin pairs and the bound \texorpdfstring{$P - O \le 88$}{P - O <= 88}}

\begin{definition}[Origin pair, resolved file]\label{def:originpair}
The two pawns that begin on file $i$ (one White, one Black) are that file's
\emph{origin pair}. File $i$ is \emph{resolved} if at least one of those two
pawns makes a diagonal pawn move at some point in the game; otherwise it is
\emph{unresolved}. Write $f$ for the number of resolved files.
\end{definition}

A diagonal pawn move is the only way a pawn changes file, and it is always a
capture; so both pawns of an unresolved pair spend their entire lives on
file $i$.

\begin{lemma}[Unresolved pair bound]\label{lem:cap10}
The two pawns of an unresolved origin pair make at most $10$ pawn moves
combined, and $10$ is attained in a legal game.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Both pawns stay on file $i$. While both are on the board, the White pawn is
strictly below the Black one: neither can capture the other (a capture would
be diagonal), and neither can pass the other, since a single push onto an
occupied square is illegal and a double push may not jump over an occupied
square. So if White's pawn has advanced $a$ ranks and Black's $b$ ranks,
they stand on ranks $2+a < 7-b$, giving $a + b \le 4$; and since each pawn
move advances at least one rank, the two pawns have made at most $4$
combined moves while both are on the board. In particular neither pawn can
promote while the other is on the board, so at most one of the pair ever
promotes, and only after the other has been captured (necessarily by some
other piece).

When one pawn of the pair leaves the board its total contribution is frozen
at some $d \le 4$, and the survivor's lifetime total is at most $6$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:pawnbasics}, for a combined total of at most
$d + 6 \le 10$.

Ten is attained: in
\[
  \text{1.\,Nf3 a6\; 2.\,Ng1 a5\; 3.\,Nf3 a4\; 4.\,Ng1 a3\; 5.\,Nxa3 Nc6\;
  6.\,Nc4 Rb8\; 7.\,a3 Nf6\; 8.\,a4 Ng8\;}
\]
\[
  \text{9.\,a5 Nf6\; 10.\,a6 Ng8\; 11.\,a7 Nf6\; 12.\,a8=Q Ng8}
\]
Black's a-pawn walks $a7$--$a3$ in four moves and is captured there by the
knight; White's a-pawn then walks the whole file and promotes, six more.
Neither pawn ever moved diagonally, so the a-file is unresolved throughout.
The sequence is a legal prefix rather than a completed game, but in any
legal completion neither count changes --- the survivor has promoted and
the other has been captured, so the pair makes no further pawn move and
the file stays unresolved --- so a completed legal game attains ten as
well. Figure~\ref{fig:cap10} shows the position after Black's fourth pawn
move and the final position of the sequence.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:cap10caution}
We record a caution for anyone reproducing this bound. The first
observation of the proof --- ``two facing pawns cannot pass, so the pair
makes at most $4$ combined pawn moves'' --- holds only \emph{while both
pawns are on the board}; taken as an upper bound for the whole game it is
false, since capturing one pawn frees the survivor to advance.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[Overlap floor]\label{lem:overlapfloor}
$O \ge f$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For each resolved file $i$ choose one diagonal pawn move made by a pawn
whose \emph{origin} file is $i$; one exists by definition, and it is a
capture. A pawn has one origin file, so the chosen moves of distinct
resolved files have distinct movers and are distinct moves, each of them
an overlap.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:pminuso}
$P - O \le 88$, and equality forces $f = 8$, $P = 96$ and $O = 8$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The eight origin pairs partition the sixteen pawns, so summing
Lemma~\ref{lem:cap10} over unresolved pairs and the trivial bound $12$ over
resolved pairs, and combining with Lemma~\ref{lem:pawnbasics},
\[
  P \;\le\; \min\bigl(96,\; 10(8-f) + 12f\bigr) \;=\; \min(96,\, 80 + 2f).
\]
With Lemma~\ref{lem:overlapfloor},
$P - O \le \min(96, 80+2f) - f$. For $f \le 7$ this is at most
$80 + 2f - f = 80 + f \le 87$; for $f = 8$ it is $96 - 8 = 88$. The maximum
over $f$ is therefore $88$, attained only at $f = 8$ with $P = 96$ and
$O = f = 8$.
\end{proof}

Maximising over $f$ is what makes Proposition~\ref{prop:pminuso} a statement
about all games rather than about games with eight overlaps; asserting
$O \ge 8$ outright would be circular, being true only \emph{because}
$K = 118$ forces every file to be resolved.

\subsection{The bound \texorpdfstring{$C + T \le 30$}{C + T <= 30}}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:ct30}
$C + T \le 30$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Only the $30$ non-king pieces can ever be captured, so $C \le 30$. If
$C \le 29$ then, since $T \le 1$, we get $C + T \le 30$. If $C = 30$, the
$30$th capture leaves the two kings alone on the board. King versus king is
a dead position --- no sequence of legal moves mates --- so by
Article~5.2.2 the game ends on that very ply; no quiet ply follows the last
critical move and $T = 0$, giving $C + T = 30$.
\end{proof}

This is the only place any dead-position reasoning enters the upper bound,
and king versus king is the one case where deadness is beyond dispute. The
two ways of reaching $30$ are worth displaying, because they explain why
draws do not outlast checkmates:
\[
  \underbrace{96 + 30 - 8 + 0}_{\text{all $30$ captured, no closing
  segment}} \;=\; \underbrace{96 + 29 - 8 + 1}_{\text{$29$ captured, one
  closing segment}} \;=\; 118 .
\]

\subsection{The bound \texorpdfstring{$K \le 118$}{K <= 118} and its equality case}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:k118}
Every game has $K \le 118$. Moreover a game with $K = 118$ satisfies
\[
  f = 8, \qquad P = 96, \qquad O = 8, \qquad C + T = 30,
\]
so all sixteen pawns make six pawn moves each --- every one advancing
the pawn by exactly one rank --- and promote, and $C \ge 29$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:K}, Proposition~\ref{prop:pminuso} and Lemma~\ref{lem:ct30},
\[
  K = (P - O) + (C + T) \le 88 + 30 = 118 .
\]
Equality forces $P - O = 88$ and $C + T = 30$; the former pins
$(f, P, O) = (8, 96, 8)$ by the equality case of
Proposition~\ref{prop:pminuso}, and $P = 96$ makes every pawn's count
exactly $6$, whence Lemma~\ref{lem:pawnbasics} applies to each. Finally
$T \le 1$ gives $C \ge 29$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The ceiling of 17{,}699]\label{rem:ceiling}
Theorem~\ref{thm:k118} already yields the ceiling stated in
\cite{murphy2020longest}, now as a bound over all games. A game with $K \le 117$ has
$L \le 150 \cdot 117 = 17{,}550$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:decomposition}. A game
with $K = 118$ has $P = 96$, so both sides move pawns; a pawn move is a
critical move made by the pawn's owner, so endpoints of both colours
occur. Since the virtual endpoint $e_0$ is Black by convention and some
endpoint is White, the endpoint colour sequence changes at least once:
$S \ge 1$, and $L \le 150 \cdot 118 - 1 = 17{,}699$. The next section
shows that at $K = 118$ the colour must in fact change three times, which
removes the last two plies.
\end{remark}

\section{Games with 118 segments have at least four critical-move
blocks}
\label{sec:switches}

Throughout this section, fix a game with $K = 118$; by
Theorem~\ref{thm:k118} it has $P = 96$ --- \emph{every pawn makes exactly
six pawn moves} --- and $C \ge 29$. These two facts do all the work. We
emphasise at the outset that $S \ge 3$ is claimed \emph{only} under
$K = 118$; it is false in general (Remark~\ref{rem:notglobal}).

\subsection{Blocks and patterns}

The critical moves of a game, in order, form a sequence of coloured events
(the colour of a pawn move is the pawn's owner; the colour of a capture is
the capturer, i.e.\ the side that does \emph{not} own the piece captured).
The \emph{pattern} of any finite sequence of coloured events is obtained
by grouping maximal runs of equal colour into \emph{blocks} and listing
the block colours; the pattern of the critical-move sequence is the
game's \emph{critical-move pattern}, and the pattern of the endpoint
sequence is its \emph{endpoint pattern}. Here the endpoint sequence
means $(e_1, \dots, e_K)$ of Definition~\ref{def:segments}: the virtual
endpoint $e_0$ is \emph{not} part of the endpoint pattern; it is
prepended only when computing the switch count $S$. Counting that
virtual Black endpoint, a game whose endpoint pattern consists of $n$
blocks has $S = n - 1$ if the pattern opens with Black and $S = n$ if it
opens with White. And since the endpoint sequence is the critical-move
sequence with, when $T = 1$, one element appended --- which either
extends the last block or starts a new one --- the endpoint pattern has
at least as many blocks as the critical-move pattern.

A game with $K = 118$ has at least $117$ critical moves, so its
critical-move pattern is nonempty. We refute every nonempty alternating
pattern of at most three blocks --- there are exactly six:
\[
  \mathsf{B}, \qquad \mathsf{W}, \qquad \mathsf{BW}, \qquad \mathsf{WB},
  \qquad \mathsf{BWB}, \qquad \mathsf{WBW}.
\]
Each side has $15$ capturable pieces, of which exactly $7$ (queen, two
rooks, two bishops, two knights) did not begin as pawns; the count below is
over pieces in the sense of Section~\ref{sec:k118}, so a piece that began
as a pawn and is captured after promoting still counts against the pawn
origins.

\subsection{Four patterns are eliminated by counting}

\begin{proposition}[Single block]\label{prop:oneblock}
Patterns $\mathsf{B}$ and $\mathsf{W}$ are impossible at $K = 118$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A pawn move is a critical move of the pawn's own colour. In a single-block
game only one colour makes critical moves, so only its eight pawns ever
move: $P \le 48 < 96$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Two blocks]\label{prop:twoblocks}
Patterns $\mathsf{BW}$ and $\mathsf{WB}$ are impossible at $K = 118$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write the pattern $(X, Y)$: every critical move of $X$ precedes every
critical move of $Y$. Write $C_X$ and $C_Y$ for the numbers of captures
made by $X$ and by $Y$, so that $C_X + C_Y = C$. Since $Y$ captures only
$X$'s pieces,
$C_Y \le 15$, so $C_X \ge C - 15 \ge 29 - 15 = 14$: the colour moving
first makes at least $14$ captures, all of them before $Y$ has made a
single critical move. Each piece so captured is a $Y$ piece that, being
off the board before every $Y$ critical move, never made a pawn move. At
most $7$ of the captured pieces did not begin as pawns, so at least $7$
of $Y$'s pieces that began as pawns make no pawn move at all, and
\[
  P \;\le\; 96 - 7 \cdot 6 \;=\; 54 \;<\; 96. \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

Note that $Y$ may well have made \emph{quiet} moves in the meantime; the
argument only needs its critical moves to come later.

\subsection{The home-rank lemma}

The three-block patterns are not eliminated by counting alone; the
following lemma closes both.

\begin{lemma}[Home-rank lemma]\label{lem:homerank}
Fix colours $A$ (attacker) and $D$ (defender), and consider any position
reachable from the initial position by a sequence of moves satisfying
\begin{itemize}
  \item[\textup{(R1)}] every move by $D$ is quiet, and
  \item[\textup{(R2)}] no move by $A$ captures a $D$ pawn.
\end{itemize}
Then every square of $D$'s pawn home rank still holds the $D$ pawn that
started there; no $A$ pawn ever stands on that rank; and no $A$ pawn has
made more than four pawn moves.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We use only the following elementary facts about legal chess moves,
labelled for reference: \textup{[H0]} the initial position has eight pawns on each home
rank; \textup{[H1]} a legal move starts on a square holding a piece of the
side to move; \textup{[H2]} a move onto an occupied square is a capture of
an enemy piece standing there; \textup{[H3]} castling changes only
back-rank squares; \textup{[H4]} an en passant capture removes a pawn from
rank $4$ or rank $5$; \textup{[H5]} a move changes the occupancy of no
squares other than its origin and destination, the castling rook's two
squares, and the square of the pawn captured en passant; \textup{[H6]} a pawn move
advances the pawn exactly one rank, or exactly two from its own home rank
only.

\emph{Part 1: $D$'s home rank never changes.} Induction on moves, the base
case being [H0]. Let the invariant hold and let $m$ be a move permitted by
(R1), (R2). By [H5] it suffices to rule out each way $m$ could touch a
home-rank square. $m$ cannot \emph{start} there: the square holds a $D$
pawn, only $D$ may move it [H1], and that would be a pawn move, excluded by
(R1). $m$ cannot \emph{end} there: the square is occupied, so the move
would capture an enemy piece standing there [H2]; $D$ capturing is excluded
by (R1), and the occupant is a $D$ pawn, so $A$ capturing it is excluded by
(R2). Castling touches only back ranks [H3]. En passant removes a pawn from
rank $4$ or $5$ [H4], not from a home rank (and the pawn captured would in
any case have just made a double push --- for a $D$ pawn a pawn move,
excluded by (R1)).

\emph{Part 2: no $A$ pawn reaches $D$'s home rank.} Standing there requires
a move ending there, ruled out above.

\emph{Part 3: at most four moves.} By [H6] a pawn never moves backwards and
double-pushes only from its own home rank. By Part 2 an $A$ pawn never
occupies $D$'s home rank; nor the rank beyond it, which could only be
reached by a single step from $D$'s home rank or a double push from a rank
that is not the pawn's home rank. Its range is therefore the four ranks
between its own home rank and $D$'s, and each pawn move spends at least one
of them: at most four moves. (Four is attained, by four single pushes.)
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Three blocks]\label{prop:threeblocks}
Neither $\mathsf{BWB}$ nor $\mathsf{WBW}$ is possible as the
critical-move pattern of a game with $K = 118$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write either pattern as $(X, Y, X)$, where $X$ and $Y$ are the two
colours, and write $C_X$, $C_Y$ for the numbers of captures made by $X$
and by $Y$; thus every critical move of $Y$ lies in the middle block.
Let $\tau$ be the ply of $Y$'s first critical move, and call the plies
before $\tau$ the \emph{first phase}.

Since $X$ can capture only the $15$ capturable pieces of $Y$, we have
$C_X \le 15$, so
\[
  C_Y \;=\; C - C_X \;\ge\; 29 - 15 \;=\; 14 .
\]
All these captures are critical moves of $Y$ and therefore occur in the
middle block. At most seven of the pieces captured by $Y$ did not begin
as pawns, so at least seven of them are $X$-pieces that began as pawns.

Fix one such piece $p$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:k118} it makes exactly six
pawn moves, all before its capture. Every pawn move of $p$ is a critical
move of $X$, and a critical move of $X$ at a ply $> \tau$ belongs to the
final $X$-block, hence comes after \emph{every} critical move of $Y$ ---
in particular after the move on which $p$ is captured, which is
impossible for a piece no longer on the board. So all six of its pawn
moves occur in the first phase.

Now apply Lemma~\ref{lem:homerank} to the first phase with $A = X$ and
$D = Y$. (R1) holds by the definition of $\tau$: every move by $Y$
before $\tau$ is quiet. For (R2), suppose a move by $X$ in the first
phase captured a $Y$ pawn. That pawn would be off the board before
$Y$'s first critical move; since each of its pawn moves would itself be
a critical move of $Y$, it would make no pawn move at all,
contradicting $P = 96$. So (R2) holds as well. The lemma bounds every
$X$ pawn by four pawn moves in the first phase --- but each of the
seven pieces fixed above needs six there. Counting only the shortfall
of two for each,
\[
  P \;\le\; 96 - 7 \cdot (6 - 4) \;=\; 82 \;<\; 96. \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

The only escape the home-rank lemma allows --- capturing a way through the
home rank --- costs the captured pawn all six of its moves, which
Theorem~\ref{thm:k118} cannot spare. That is the entire content of clause
(R2) above.

\begin{theorem}[Four blocks]\label{thm:s3}
Every game with $K = 118$ has a critical-move pattern of at least four
blocks; hence its endpoint pattern also has at least four blocks, and
$S \ge 3$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The critical-move pattern is a nonempty alternating pattern, and the
six such patterns of at most three blocks are refuted by
Propositions~\ref{prop:oneblock}, \ref{prop:twoblocks}
and~\ref{prop:threeblocks}; so it has at least four blocks, and by the
appending observation of Section~\ref{sec:switches} so does the
endpoint pattern. A game whose endpoint pattern has $n \ge 4$ blocks
has $S \ge n - 1 \ge 3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:notglobal}
$S \ge 3$ is conditional on $K = 118$, and only on it: every step above
runs on $P = 96$ and $C \ge 29$. It is plainly false in general. The
16-ply game \textup{1.\,Nf3 Nf6 2.\,Ng1 Ng8} repeated four times is legal
and ends in fivefold repetition of the initial position; it has no pawn
move and no capture, so $K = 1$ (the single closing segment), and its one
endpoint is a Black ply, matching the virtual Black opening: $S = 0$. This
costs nothing in Section~\ref{sec:main}: a game with $K \le 117$ forfeits
at least $150$ plies, more than any switch count could recover.
\end{remark}

\section{The maximum length}\label{sec:main}

\begin{theorem}[Upper bound]\label{thm:main}
No legal chess game lasts more than $17{,}697$ plies.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let a game have $K$ segments. If $K \le 117$ then by
Lemma~\ref{lem:decomposition} with the trivial $S \ge 0$,
\[
  L \;\le\; 150 \cdot 117 \;=\; 17{,}550 \;<\; 17{,}697 .
\]
If $K = 118$ --- the largest value Theorem~\ref{thm:k118} permits --- then
Theorem~\ref{thm:s3} gives $S \ge 3$, so
\[
  L \;\le\; 150 \cdot 118 - 3 \;=\; 17{,}697 . \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

The two cases must be kept separate, because $S \ge 3$ is not available at
$K \le 117$ (Remark~\ref{rem:notglobal}); it is also not needed there,
since one whole segment outweighs any possible switch count.

The equality analysis costs nothing further, and it determines a
maximum-length game rather precisely:

\begin{corollary}[Structure of maximum-length games]\label{cor:structure}
A game of length $17{,}697$ satisfies
\[
  K = 118, \qquad S = 3, \qquad \delta_i = 0 \text{ for every } i,
\]
and therefore, by the equality case of Theorem~\ref{thm:k118},
\[
  f = 8, \qquad P = 96, \qquad O = 8, \qquad C + T = 30 .
\]
In words: all sixteen pawns make six pawn moves each --- every one
advancing the pawn by exactly one rank --- and promote, at least $29$
captures occur, every one of the $118$ segments
attains its target length ($150$ plies, or $149$ across a colour change),
and both the critical-move pattern and the endpoint pattern are
$\mathsf{BWBW}$: Black makes the first critical move, and the final
endpoint is White's.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:k118}, $K \le 118$; if $K \le 117$ the game has at
most $17{,}550$ plies, so $K = 118$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:s3} gives
$S \ge 3$. The decomposition of Lemma~\ref{lem:decomposition} then reads
\[
  17{,}697 \;=\; 150 \cdot 118 - S - \sum_{i=1}^{118} \delta_i
  \;\le\; 150 \cdot 118 - 3 \;=\; 17{,}697,
\]
with equality throughout: $S = 3$ and every $\delta_i = 0$. The equality
case of Theorem~\ref{thm:k118} supplies $f$, $P$, $O$ and $C + T$, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:pawnbasics} the behaviour of each pawn.

For the patterns, Theorem~\ref{thm:s3} gives at least four blocks in
the critical-move pattern. Since $S = 3$, there can be no more than
four: each change between consecutive critical-move blocks is an
adjacent unequal pair of the endpoint sequence, already counted by $S$.
Thus the critical-move pattern has exactly four blocks. It cannot open
with White: the initial comparison with the virtual Black endpoint
$e_0$ would then add one switch to the three changes between the four
blocks, giving $S \ge 4$. Hence the critical-move pattern is
$\mathsf{BWBW}$. Finally, a terminal endpoint, if present, cannot start
a new block, since that would add a fourth switch; therefore the
endpoint pattern is also $\mathsf{BWBW}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Exact maximum]\label{cor:exact}
The maximum length of a chess game under the FIDE Laws is exactly
$17{,}697$ plies --- $8{,}848$ full moves and one final White ply.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is attained: Murphy's explicit game
\cite{murphy2020longest} is $17{,}697$ plies long and ends in checkmate
on White's $8{,}849$th move, as independently re-verified, ply by ply,
in Appendix~\ref{app:witness}, where the game is also matched against
Corollary~\ref{cor:structure}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:witnessprofile}
The reference game realises Corollary~\ref{cor:structure} with $C = 29$
and $T = 1$: its final ply is a quiet
checkmate delivered with the halfmove clock at exactly $150$, the
checkmate taking precedence under Article~9.6.2. Improving on such a game is
impossible rather than merely hard: it fills every segment, and its three
lost plies are exactly the three switches Theorem~\ref{thm:s3} proves
unavoidable.
\end{remark}

\section{The reference game}\label{app:witness}

Among the published maximum-length games we verify the one constructed
by hand
in \cite{murphy2020longest} --- the \emph{reference game}; Labelle's page
\cite{labelle2015longest} supplies another, machine-generated. (The
repository's documentation and \texttt{make} targets call the reference
game the \emph{witness}.) The companion repository \cite{repo} contains
an independent verifier for it, built on the move generation of the
\texttt{python-chess} library \cite{pythonchess} and this project's own
implementation of the termination rules of Section~\ref{sec:rules}: it
replays the game from the initial position, rejects any move that is
illegal or played after the game has ended, classifies the position
after every ply, and emits a per-ply trace (clock value, position
multiplicity, termination state); a sequence passes only if the game
ends exactly on its last move. The verified facts:

\begin{itemize}
  \item The game is legal, $17{,}697$ plies long, and ends in checkmate,
    in the final position
    \texttt{3k4/3Q4/4K3/8/8/8/8/8} --- king and queen against king,
    Figure~\ref{fig:witnessfinal}.
  \item No automatic termination fires early. The halfmove clock stays
    below $150$ after every ply but the last; no position ever occurs even
    three times (the maximum multiplicity of any position over the whole
    game, under the identity of Article~9.2, is \emph{two}), so
    fivefold repetition is never approached; no stalemate occurs.
  \item No position of the game is a dead position, by an argument that needs no
    material test: from every position, the remaining moves of the game
    are themselves a legal series ending in checkmate, and a position from
    which checkmate is reachable is precisely what Article~5.2.2 does not
    end. The verifier checks the legality of those suffixes.
  \item The final ply is a quiet checkmate played with the halfmove clock
    at exactly $150$: the game ends on the precedence clause of
    Article~9.6.2. Its length is insensitive to that clause --- under
    either reading the $17{,}697$th ply is played and the game ends on it;
    the clause decides only whether the result is a win or a draw.
  \item The profile is the one Corollary~\ref{cor:structure} forces:
    $117$ critical moves plus a closing segment ($K = 118$), with
    $P = 96$ (each of the sixteen pawns makes exactly six pawn moves),
    $C = 29$, $O = 8$ (every file resolved), $T = 1$. The endpoint
    pattern is $\mathsf{BWBW}$, so $S = 3$, and the decomposition
    of Lemma~\ref{lem:decomposition} closes exactly:
    $115$ segments of $150$ plies, $3$ segments of $149$ (one per switch),
    and $\sum_i \delta_i = 0$:
    \[
      17{,}697 \;=\; 150 \cdot 118 - 3 - 0 .
    \]
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.42\textwidth]{figures/witness-final}
  \caption{The final position of the reference game, after ply
  $17{,}697$: checkmate, delivered with the halfmove clock at exactly
  $150$.}
  \label{fig:witnessfinal}
\end{figure}

Two points of this verifier are rules of chess rather than engineering
choices, and the reference game exercises both. First, the order of the
termination tests: checkmate is tested before the $150$th-quiet-ply
test, because the final ply above is simultaneously both, and the
precedence clause of Article~9.6.2 says the mate stands --- a verifier
that consults the clock first accepts the same $17{,}697$ plies but
scores the game a draw. (The verifier's material-draw screen is not a
decision procedure for Article~5.2.2 --- for such a procedure see
\cite{ambrona2022}; it is never load-bearing here, by the suffix
argument above.) Second, position identity for repetition: following
Article~9.2, two positions count as the same exactly when their piece
placement, the side to move, the \emph{retained} castling rights ---
whether each side still has the right to castle with each original
rook, regardless of whether castling is legal in the current position
--- and the availability of a \emph{legal}
en passant capture all agree. Naive position keys fail in both
directions. Comparing whole FEN strings separates positions the Laws
identify: the halfmove clock and the move number play no part in
identity, and the en passant target is recorded after every double push
whether or not a capture is actually legal --- so the fivefold rule
would never fire. Comparing only piece placement and the side to move
merges positions the Laws distinguish, since retained castling rights
and a legal en passant capture also matter. The verifier keys its
repetition table on exactly the four data above; under that identity,
no position of the reference game ever occurs three times. The verifier
is deliberately independent: it
imports nothing else from the repository, so the judge shares no code
with any producer of games.

The repository also \emph{rebuilds} a different game of
the same length --- same $118$ critical moves, freshly generated quiet
filler; $17{,}032$ of the $17{,}697$ plies differ --- and verifies it the
same way, so attainment does not rest on a single artefact.

\section{Computational cross-checks}\label{app:crosschecks}

Every finite case analysis in the text, each also proved by hand, was
exhausted by computer: the unresolved-pair bound of
Lemma~\ref{lem:cap10}, over a state space strictly more permissive than
chess (turn order ignored, no third piece blocking the file, either pawn
removable at any moment without cost); the impossibility of two facing
pawns passing each other on a file; the uniqueness of the equality
assignment of Theorem~\ref{thm:k118} (the same enumeration at $K = 117$
admits many assignments, which is what makes the pinning at $118$
informative); the terminal-endpoint step of Theorem~\ref{thm:s3} and
Corollary~\ref{cor:structure} --- appending the terminal endpoint
cannot lower the block count, and starting a new block adds a switch
--- over every alternating pattern of up to twelve blocks; and, for
Lemma~\ref{lem:homerank}, the finite obligations [H0], [H3] and [H4].
The universally quantified obligations [H1], [H2], [H5], [H6] admit no
enumeration: they are read off the Laws \cite{fide2023laws}, and the
repository checks that the move generator we rely on respects them over
a large randomised corpus of positions --- a check on the tooling, not
on the mathematics.

To reduce the risk of an undetected error in the hand argument of
Section~\ref{sec:switches}, the question ``does any endpoint pattern of
at most three blocks admit $K = 118$?'' is cross-checked by two
additional computational methods: a CP-SAT constraint model \cite{ortools}, and a solver-free
arithmetic checker that declares its own constants, imposes aggregated
forms of the same conditions --- each likewise a necessary condition of
legality --- and decides the same question by direct enumeration.

Fix an alternating endpoint pattern $\sigma$, an ending
$e \in \{\text{checkmate}, \text{draw}\}$, and a target $k$. The model
$M(\sigma, e, k)$ is a finite system of constraints over bounded integer
variables: per-pawn pawn-move counts in each block of the pawn's colour,
with a per-pawn total of at most six; per-piece capture indicators, each
captured piece assigned the enemy-colour block in which it is captured, a
captured piece that began as a pawn making no pawn move in any later
block; capture, overlap
and closing-segment totals with the coupling of Proposition~\ref{prop:pminuso} imposed through
a free resolved-file count; the bound of Lemma~\ref{lem:homerank} on
opening-block pawn moves, imposed only while no enemy pawn has been
captured in that block; for $e = \text{checkmate}$, the requirement that
the side owning the final block retain at least one non-king piece; and the
requirement that the totals compose to $k$ as in \eqref{eq:K}.

\emph{Soundness property.} Let $G$ be a legal game with $K(G) = k$ whose
endpoint pattern is $\sigma$, and set $e$ to checkmate if $G$ ends
in mate and to draw otherwise. Then the observed values of $G$ --- its
per-pawn, per-block move counts, its capture blocks, and its totals ---
satisfy every constraint of $M(\sigma, e, k)$. This holds because each
constraint is individually a necessary condition of legality; the
constraint-by-constraint argument is recorded in the repository's model
audit, and the attribution of the mating side to the final block is sound
precisely because $\sigma$ is a pattern of \emph{segment endpoints}
(Definition~\ref{def:segments}), the mate being the last endpoint.
Consequently, if $M(\sigma, e, 118)$ is infeasible for both values of $e$,
then no legal game with endpoint pattern $\sigma$ attains $K = 118$. No
claim is made in the converse direction: a satisfying assignment need not
correspond to any legal game, and no conclusion of this paper rests on a
feasibility verdict.

Coverage is finite. An alternating pattern of $n \ge 5$ blocks has $S \ge 4$
by the block count of Section~\ref{sec:switches}, so the sixteen decided
instances --- the eight endpoint patterns of at most four blocks, under
both endings
--- include every pattern that the refutations of
Section~\ref{sec:switches} concern. CP-SAT decides
each finite instance exactly; a solver timeout is surfaced as an error and
never reported as infeasibility. The results, identical under both
endings: all six endpoint patterns of at most three blocks are
infeasible, and the model is feasible exactly for $\mathsf{BWBW}$ and
$\mathsf{WBWB}$ --- in agreement with Theorem~\ref{thm:s3}, whose
endpoint half asserts precisely that no endpoint pattern of at most
three blocks occurs at $K = 118$. (The propositions of
Section~\ref{sec:switches} refute critical-move patterns; the theorem
carries the refutation over to endpoint patterns, which are what the
model decides.) Removing
the home-rank constraint changes exactly $\mathsf{BWB}$ and
$\mathsf{WBW}$ from infeasible to feasible and leaves every other verdict
unchanged, confirming that the lemma carries precisely the two cases
Proposition~\ref{prop:threeblocks} assigns to it and that no other
refutation of Section~\ref{sec:switches} depends on it.

The soundness property cannot be established by testing, but violations
of it can be exhibited: the constraint model accepts the exact
assignment induced by the reference game of Appendix~\ref{app:witness},
and both deciders return feasible for its endpoint pattern
$\mathsf{BWBW}$. Three constraints that were, at stages of this work,
stronger than legality --- the first of them the misreading against
which Remark~\ref{rem:cap10caution} warns --- were each found by the
constraint-by-constraint audit, repaired, and pinned by regression
tests; none survives in the verdicts above, and the full record, with
explicit refuting games, is in the repository.

What a reader of these checks must trust: the summary of the Laws in
Section~\ref{sec:rules}, in particular that Articles 9.6.1--9.6.2 end
games automatically and that mate takes precedence on the final ply; and
the move generation of \texttt{python-chess} \cite{pythonchess} (version
pinned in the repository), which underlies both the game verification
and the corpus checks --- the obligations [H1], [H2], [H5], [H6] are
facts about chess that we verify the library respects, not facts we
derive from it. The reference game is an artefact produced by others
\cite{murphy2020longest} and accepted by our verifier, separating
producer from judge; a verification under an independently implemented
move generator would shrink this trust further.
The claims-to-code map (\texttt{CLAIMS.md}), invocation commands, corpus
and search statistics, the byte-for-byte--reproducible certificate and
the full audit record live in the companion repository \cite{repo}; the
repository version corresponding to this paper is tag \texttt{v1.0.0},
archived at the DOI cited there.
